Working Glossary Note

Purpose:

This note tracks terms that are likely to need controlled definitions in the paper because they carry different meanings across disciplines or are easy to misuse in interdisciplinary writing.

Terms likely to need explicit definition:

- morphology
- reconstruction
- proxy task
- transfer
- hallucination
- legibility
- readability
- artifact
- substrate
- degradation
- validation
- signal
- generalization
- interpolation
- stratigraphy
- document-level split
- sampler weighting

Why this matters:

In an interdisciplinary paper, readers may silently import the meaning of a term from their own field. That can create confusion even when the sentence is technically correct within one discipline. The paper should therefore define key terms in the sense intended by this project, especially where the terms may differ across paleography, papyrology, machine learning, linguistics, image processing, and mathematics.

Initial drafting principle:

- define a term once in the main text when it first becomes important
- use a footnote only if the term is especially overloaded or the clarification would interrupt the sentence
- avoid using technical terms loosely if a more ordinary phrase would do

Example of the problem:

The word "morphology" may suggest:

- grammatical word formation in linguistics
- letterform shape and scribal variation in paleography
- geometric or structural form in a broader mathematical or image-analysis sense

If the paper uses "morphology" to mean the visual form of letter shapes, that should be stated explicitly.

Recommended next step:

Work through these terms one by one and produce:

1. a plain-language explanation for internal use
2. a paper-appropriate definition or footnote where needed

Working Definitions

Morphology

Plain-language:
In this project, "morphology" refers to the visual form of the letters themselves: their shape, stroke proportions, curvature, terminals, open or closed forms, and scribal variation within a letter class. It does not mean grammatical morphology in the linguistic sense, nor does it refer to morphology in the more abstract mathematical or image-processing sense of shape transformation.

Paper-ready definition:
"In this paper, 'morphology' refers to the visual form of letter shapes, including stroke structure, curvature, terminal forms, and scribal variation within a letter class. It is used neither in the linguistic sense of word formation nor in the more abstract mathematical or image-processing sense of morphological transformation."

Reconstruction

Plain-language:
In this project, "reconstruction" may refer to more than one thing. It can mean papyrological or material reconstruction of fragments, layers, or scroll surfaces; it can mean image reconstruction in the machine-learning sense, where a degraded input is mapped to a cleaner target; or it can mean interpretive reconstruction of missing text or structure. These senses should not be conflated.

Paper-ready definition:
"In this paper, 'reconstruction' is used in more than one sense and should be read carefully from context. In papyrological discussion, it refers to the recovery of the original structural relationships of fragments, layers, or scroll surfaces. In the machine-learning pipeline, it refers more narrowly to an image-prediction task in which a degraded input is mapped to a cleaner target representation. These senses should not be treated as equivalent."

Hallucination

Plain-language:
Here, "hallucination" does not primarily mean free-form invented text. It refers to visually plausible but unsupported output: apparent letterforms, traces, or reconstructions that do not correspond to genuine underlying ink signal, but instead arise from artifact, leakage, noise, or overinterpretation.

Paper-ready definition:
"In this paper, 'hallucination' does not refer primarily to free-form linguistic invention in the sense familiar from large language models. Rather, it refers to visually plausible but unsupported output: apparent letterforms, traces, or reconstructions that do not correspond to genuine underlying ink signal, but instead arise from artifact, leakage, noise, or overinterpretation."

Proxy Task

Plain-language:
A proxy task is not the real target task. It is a stand-in task that is easier to define, supervise, or train on, and that is hoped to teach something useful for the real problem. In this project, the proxy task is training on curated uncial exemplars under supervised objectives, while the real task is detecting genuine ink and contributing to legibility on real grayscale images from virtually unrolled papyrus.

Paper-ready definition:
"The present training regime should be understood as a proxy task rather than as the final task itself. The model is trained on curated uncial exemplars under supervised objectives that are intended to teach useful visual priors, but the decisive question is whether any of that learned behavior transfers to the much harder problem of detecting genuine ink signal in novel grayscale imagery from virtually unrolled papyrus."

Transfer

Plain-language:
"Transfer" refers to whether what the model learns in one setting remains useful in another, harder, or more relevant setting. In this project, the source setting is curated uncial training data and the target setting is novel grayscale imagery from virtually unrolled papyrus.

Paper-ready definition:
"In this paper, 'transfer' refers to the extent to which behavior learned on the supervised training regime carries over to the substantially harder target setting of novel grayscale imagery from virtually unrolled papyrus. The central question is therefore not only whether the model performs well on the training proxy, but whether what it learns remains useful under conditions that differ materially from that proxy."

Legibility

Plain-language:
"Legibility" refers to whether visual traces can be recognized as meaningful letterforms or parts of letterforms at all. It is more local and visual than full reading.

Paper-ready definition:
"'Legibility' refers here to the extent to which visual traces can be recognized as meaningful letterforms or parts of letterforms, even before a full or reliable reading is possible."

Readability

Plain-language:
"Readability" is a stronger condition than legibility. It refers to whether traces can be interpreted as coherent text rather than merely as isolated or locally plausible letterforms.

Paper-ready definition:
"'Readability' refers here to the extent to which visual traces can be interpreted as coherent text rather than merely as isolated or locally plausible letterforms. It therefore requires more than local visual recognition and comes closer to a defensible act of reading."

Relationship:
Legibility is necessary for readability, but not sufficient for it.

Artifact

Plain-language:
An artifact is a visually meaningful but misleading feature produced by damage, imaging, digital reconstruction, virtual unwrapping, or model output rather than by genuine underlying ink or writing. Artifacts may resemble letterforms or strokes closely enough to invite overinterpretation.

Paper-ready definition:
"In this paper, an 'artifact' refers to a visually meaningful but misleading feature produced by damage, imaging, digital reconstruction, virtual unwrapping, or model output rather than by genuine underlying ink. Artifacts may resemble letterforms or strokes closely enough to invite overinterpretation."

Signal

Plain-language:
"Signal" refers to visual information that genuinely derives from underlying ink or writing rather than from substrate texture, damage, imaging effects, digital reconstruction artifacts, or model-generated distortions.

Paper-ready definition:
"In this paper, 'signal' refers to visual information that genuinely derives from underlying ink or writing rather than from substrate texture, damage, imaging effects, digital reconstruction artifacts, or model-generated distortions. A central challenge is that genuine signal may be faint or partial, while non-signal features may appear more visually prominent."

Validation

Plain-language:
In this project, validation has at least two levels. Narrowly, it means performance on held-out supervised data. More broadly, it must also include whether the learned behavior remains credible under the much harder domain shift posed by real scroll imagery.

Paper-ready definition:
"In this paper, 'validation' should not be understood only in the narrow machine-learning sense of performance on held-out supervised data. That form of validation remains necessary, but it is not sufficient for the present problem. Because the ultimate target domain differs substantially from the curated training regime, meaningful validation must also include evidence about transfer to more realistic grayscale scroll imagery."

Generalization

Plain-language:
"Generalization" refers to the model's ability to remain useful on new data drawn from the same or a closely related distribution as the training corpus, such as held-out documents from the uncial dataset. It is not the same as transfer to the much harder scroll domain.

Paper-ready definition:
"In this paper, 'generalization' refers to the model's ability to remain useful on new data drawn from the same or a closely related distribution as the training corpus, such as held-out documents from the uncial dataset. This should be distinguished from transfer, which concerns usefulness under the much larger domain shift posed by real scroll imagery."

Document-Level Split

Plain-language:
A "document-level split" means that training and validation are separated by source document rather than by random sample alone. If several images come from the same papyrus document, they all go to training or all go to validation, but not both.

Paper-ready definition:
"A 'document-level split' refers to a train/validation partition in which samples are grouped by source document rather than assigned independently at random. Its purpose is to reduce leakage between highly related samples and to provide a more defensible test of whether learned behavior extends beyond one document-specific context."

Sampler Weighting

Plain-language:
"Sampler weighting" changes how often different samples are shown during training without changing the underlying dataset itself. In this project, mild inverse-square-root weighting gives rarer classes somewhat more exposure while avoiding a completely artificial uniform distribution.

Paper-ready definition:
"Sampler weighting" refers here to adjusting the probability with which training samples are drawn, without altering the underlying corpus itself. In the present pipeline, inverse-square-root weighting is used as a mild correction for imbalance, so that rarer classes contribute more consistently to training without replacing the original class distribution with an artificial uniform one.

Degradation

Plain-language:
"Degradation" means deliberately making an image harder to read in controlled ways. In the revised pipeline, clean uncial exemplars are degraded to produce more difficult inputs while the clean image remains the target.

Paper-ready definition:
"Degradation" refers here to the controlled corruption of otherwise cleaner source images in order to create more difficult inputs for training. In the revised pipeline, degradation is applied to real uncial exemplars so that the model learns from authentic morphology while still being exposed to variation in contrast, blur, rotation, and noise.

Interpolation

Plain-language:
"Interpolation" refers to the estimation of new pixel values when an image is resized, warped, aligned, or digitally reconstructed. It can alter the appearance of faint traces and is not just a trivial implementation detail.

Paper-ready definition:
"Interpolation" refers to the estimation of new pixel values when an image is resized, warped, aligned, or digitally reconstructed. In the present context, interpolation is not merely a low-level implementation detail, because it can affect the apparent continuity, sharpness, or visibility of faint traces and therefore influence both human and model-based interpretation."

Substrate

Plain-language:
"Substrate" refers to the material surface or physical support on which writing appears, or from which its traces must be distinguished. In this project, that usually means the papyrus itself, including its fibers, texture, compression patterns, damage, and other material features that can be mistaken for ink or can obscure genuine ink signal.

Paper-ready definition:
"In this paper, 'substrate' refers to the material support of the writing, especially the papyrus surface and its physical features, including fiber structure, texture, compression, and damage patterns. The term is used to distinguish the material background from the ink signal that the model is intended to detect."

Stratigraphy

Plain-language:
"Stratigraphy" refers to the layered structure and spatial relationships of overlapping material surfaces. In the context of carbonized and virtually unwrapped scrolls, it concerns how adjacent layers, folds, or compressed surfaces relate to one another and how those relationships affect interpretation of visible traces.

Paper-ready definition:
"In this paper, 'stratigraphy' refers to the layered structural relationships among adjacent or overlapping material surfaces within the scroll. The term is important because traces that appear visually contiguous in an image may in fact derive from different layers or neighboring surfaces, and their interpretation therefore depends in part on the reconstructed layering of the object."
